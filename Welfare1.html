Step 1 · The space

Two people, two axes

Everything happens in one picture of a two-person society — a “rich” person and a “poor” person. The horizontal axis is the rich person’s welfare; moving right makes them better off, left worse off. The vertical axis is the poor person’s welfare; moving up makes them better off, down worse off.

Point s is the status quo — the split the market produces on its own. It sits low and to the right: the rich are well ahead of the poor. Every policy is judged by where it moves society relative to s. The two faint grey lines through s just mark “no change” for each person.

Step 2 · The equality line

Where rich and poor are equal

The solid line runs at 45° from the origin. Every point on it is a society where the rich and poor have exactly equal welfare. Watch the teal dot slide along it — both people’s welfare changes by the same amount, so the gap stays zero while the total (the meter under the chart) grows or shrinks. Equality is preserved; the pie just changes size.

s sits well below and to the right of this line: that gap is the market’s inequality.

Step 3 · The constant-inequality line

Holding inequality fixed

The dotted line is the equality line’s twin: it runs parallel to it, but passes through s. Every point on it describes a society with the same rich–poor gap as s — the same level of inequality, just a bigger or smaller pie.

Watch the dot slide along it — both bars rise and fall together, so the Gap bar stays fixed while the Total changes. Where the equality line pins the gap at zero, this one pins it at exactly the market’s level — the benchmark for “grew the pie without touching inequality.”

Step 4 · Helping everyone

The Pareto region

Use the two grey guides through s. Anything in the shaded upper-right box makes both people better off than they were at s — the rich gain (rightward) and the poor gain (upward). Economists call a move that helps someone while harming no one a Pareto improvement.

This box is the heart of the “public-economics” reading of the welfare state: much of what it does isn’t taking from one to give to another — it’s finding moves that leave everyone ahead.

Step 5 · Point a

A pure efficiency gain

Point a sits right on that constant-inequality line, up and to the right of s — so both gain and the rich–poor gap is unchanged. A “win-win that doesn’t touch inequality.”

Both gainGap unchanged
Policy example
A public pension that pools longevity risk. Nobody knows how long they’ll live — life expectancy at 65 has a standard deviation of roughly 15 years — so people can’t know how much to save. A collective pension is essentially a life annuity: it insures against “outliving your savings” and smooths consumption across the life cycle. Because private annuity markets are crippled by adverse selection, public provision can make everyone better off without changing who is rich or poor. Heath, The Machinery of Government (2020), pp. 174–175; Barr, The Welfare State as Piggy Bank (2001), ch. 1 (the “piggy-bank” function: insurance & consumption smoothing, “pensions being a particularly important example”).
Step 6 · Point b

A win-win that also narrows the gap

Point b is still in the Pareto box — both gain — but it sits above the dotted line, closer to the equality line. The poor gain proportionally more, so inequality falls at the same time as everyone wins.

Both gainMore equal
Policy example
Health insurance financed through a progressive income tax. Public provision overcomes the market failures that plague private health insurance, so it is an efficiency gain for all (a Pareto improvement). But because it is funded progressively, the poor receive coverage on far better terms than they could buy privately — so inequality narrows too. Heath’s key point: it is the Pareto improvement, not the redistribution, that justifies the state’s involvement. Heath, The Machinery of Government (2020), pp. 175–176.
Step 7 · The zero-net-welfare line

When does total welfare rise?

Add up the two people’s welfare and you get total welfare. The dashed orange line collects every society with the same total as s: it slopes down at 45°, because one unit gained by the poor is exactly cancelled by one unit lost to the rich.

Watch the orange dot slide along it — the poor’s bar and the rich’s bar trade off, but the Total bar never moves. Cross to the upper-right of the line and total welfare rises; fall to the lower-left and it drops. This is the test for the redistributions that follow.

Step 8 · Point c

Redistribution that grows the pie

Now we leave the Pareto box. Point c is up and to the left of s: the poor gain, but the rich lose — genuine redistribution. Yet c sits on the net-gain side of the orange line, so the poor’s gain outweighs the rich’s loss and total welfare still rises.

RedistributiveMore equalWelfare-maximizing
Policy example
A cash transfer from rich to poor where a dollar is worth more to the recipient than to the donor (income has diminishing marginal value) and the program is cheap to run with few work-disincentive losses. The transfer shrinks the gap and raises the combined welfare of the two. This is the “Robin Hood” function done well. Heath, The Machinery of Government (2020), p. 175 (c is “welfare-maximizing, because the gain to poor is larger than the loss to rich”); Barr, The Welfare State as Piggy Bank (2001), ch. 1 (the “Robin Hood” function).
Step 9 · Point d

Redistribution that shrinks the pie

Point d is also redistributive — poor up, rich down — and also more equal than s. The difference is that d falls on the net-loss side of the orange line: the rich lose more than the poor gain, so total welfare drops even as the gap narrows.

RedistributiveMore equalNet loss
Policy example
A transfer with heavy administrative costs or strong work disincentives — so much of what the rich give up never reaches the poor, or discourages effort enough that the economy shrinks. Society is more equal, but smaller. Same egalitarian motive as c; worse execution. Heath, The Machinery of Government (2020), p. 175 (d is “non-welfare-maximizing, in that rich loses more than poor gains”).
Step 10 · Point e

Leveling down

Point e sits down and to the left of s: both people are worse off. It is more equal — closer to the equality line — but it got there by dragging everyone down. A move that harms everyone is Pareto-inferior; pursuing equality this way is called leveling down.

Both worse offMore equalNet loss
Policy example
The state monopolizes a service purely to prevent inequality — say, banning private alternatives — but is a less efficient provider than the market it replaced. Everyone, rich and poor, ends up with less; the only thing gained is a more equal distribution of a smaller pie. Heath uses this as the case the egalitarian model can’t tell apart from genuine welfare improvement. Heath, The Machinery of Government (2020), p. 176 (“if the state were actually a less-efficient… provider, yet exercised a monopoly… to prevent… inequalities… intervention would actually be leveling down”).
Step 11 · Two readings of the welfare state

Robin Hood vs. the piggy bank

The whole figure is now in front of you. Heath’s argument is about which direction the welfare state actually moves society from s.

The egalitarian model — Barr’s “Robin Hood” function — pictures the state pushing s toward c, d, even e: taking from the rich to give to the poor, with equality as the goal.

The public-economics view — Barr’s “piggy-bank” function — says the state mostly moves society toward a and b: insurance and consumption-smoothing that make everyone better off, with reduced inequality showing up as a preference for b rather than as the point of the exercise.

Robin Hood → c, d, ePiggy bank → a, b
The two functions
“Of the many purposes of the welfare state, two stand out: … the ‘Robin Hood’ function … [and] the ‘piggy-bank’ function” — insurance and redistribution over one’s own life cycle. Barr, The Welfare State as Piggy Bank (2001), ch. 1; Heath, The Machinery of Government (2020), pp. 174–176.
