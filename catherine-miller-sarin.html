Does Social Security erase the rise in wealth inequality?

Top wealth share in 1989 vs 2019 — marketable wealth alone vs including Social Security — Catherine, Miller & Sarin (2025, Journal of Finance)

Catherine, Miller & Sarin take the famous finding that US wealth has become much more concentrated since the 1980s — and ask what happens once you count the wealth most families actually rely on: their future Social Security benefits. They value accrued benefits with market yield curves in the Survey of Consumer Finances.

By the standard marketable-wealth measure (red — essentially the Saez–Zucman concept), the top 10% share climbed 62.0% → 71.5% (+9.5 pp) from 1989 to 2019. Once Social Security wealth is included (blue), the same share barely moves — 55.7% → 57.4% (+1.7 pp). The steep red slope flattens out.

Their conclusion: conventional measures, by excluding the safety net, overstate the rise in wealth concentration. This is the direct counterpoint to the Saez–Zucman wealth chart in this gallery — same starting data, opposite headline once entitlements are counted. Toggle between the top 10% and top 1%.

SCF micro-data; Social Security wealth = present value of accrued benefits (risk-free and risk-adjusted market valuations). The paper shows the attenuation is robust across 14 specifications (funding-gap benefit cuts, tax hikes, liquidity premia, heterogeneous discount rates) — all in the underlying CSV.
