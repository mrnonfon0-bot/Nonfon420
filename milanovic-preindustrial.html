How much inequality could a society sustain?

Observed Gini vs the maximum feasible Gini — 28 pre-industrial societies (Rome 14 AD onward) and their modern counterparts — Milanovic, Lindert & Williamson (2011)

Many pre-industrial societies had income Ginis in the 40s–60s, comparable to a developing country today. But a near-subsistence economy cannot sustain as much inequality: if the average person lives close to survival, there is only so much surplus an elite can take. The authors formalise this ceiling as the Inequality Possibility Frontier (IPF) — the maximum feasible Gini at a given average income.

Each dot is one society: the x-axis is average income as a multiple of subsistence (log scale); the y-axis is the observed Gini; the dashed line is the maximum feasible Gini. Pre-industrial societies (blue) hug the ceiling — they extracted roughly three-quarters of the inequality their economies could bear (mean extraction ratio ~77%). Modern counterparts (orange), far richer, sit well below their much higher ceiling (e.g. the US in 2000: a Gini of ~40 is only ~40% of the feasible maximum).

The point: the same raw Gini means something very different rich vs poor. A modest Gini in a subsistence economy can be near-total extraction; a similar Gini today leaves most feasible inequality "unused." Hover any dot for its extraction ratio.

Income on a log scale (multiple of the $PPP-300 subsistence minimum). Pre-industrial dots cluster near the dashed IPF ceiling; modern dots fall far below it.

Table 2 lists 28 pre-industrial "social tables" (Rome 14 AD → British India 1947) and 24 modern counterpart/contemporary countries (c. 1998–2004). Every plotted row was validated against the definitional identity extraction ratio = Gini / IPF; the "Average" summary rows are excluded. A few colonial cases (Moghul India 1750, Nueva España 1790) exceed 100% — the authors attribute this to measurement error / temporary below-subsistence living.

Sources — with exact citation

  • Paper: Milanovic, Branko, Peter H. Lindert & Jeffrey G. Williamson (2011). "Pre-Industrial Inequality," The Economic Journal 121(551): 255–272. doi:10.1111/j.1468-0297.2010.02403.x.
  • Data: Table 2 (pp.263–264), "Inequality Measures" — observed Gini (Gini2), mean income (multiple of subsistence), maximum feasible Gini (IPF), and inequality extraction ratio, for pre-industrial societies and modern counterparts.
  • Underlying data: historical social tables compiled by the authors; modern Ginis from Luxembourg Income Study / World Income Distribution (benchmark 2002). See gpih.ucdavis.edu.
How this chart was produced. Parsed from Table 2 (pp.263–264) text via scripts/build-milanovic.py. Every row was validated against the definitional identity extraction ratio = Gini / IPF (all 52 pass within rounding), which both selects real data rows and confirms the columns were read correctly. No pixel tracing.
