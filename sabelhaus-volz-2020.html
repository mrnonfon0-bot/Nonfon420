Does counting Social Security change US wealth concentration?

Top 10% and top 1% wealth shares, with vs without Social Security wealth, 1995–2016 — Sabelhaus & Volz (2020)

Standard wealth statistics leave out the biggest asset most families have: their future Social Security benefits. Sabelhaus & Volz estimate Social Security Wealth (SSW) — the present value of future benefits net of future taxes — for households in the Survey of Consumer Finances, and ask how counting it changes measured wealth concentration, 1995–2016.

Solid lines are wealth without Social Security (SCF net worth plus private DB pensions); dotted lines add SSW. Counting Social Security lowers the level of every top share — in 2016 it cuts the top 10% share by about 9 points (63% → 54%) — but the lines stay parallel: the top 10% comprehensive-wealth share still climbs from 45% in 1995 to 54% in 2016. Social Security makes the distribution look less unequal, but it does not reverse the rising trend.

Shown for the authors' preferred measure (person-weighted, sorted within age groups), which strips out lifecycle and marriage-pattern effects. Toggle series in the legend.

SCF micro-data; SSW uses a 2.8% real discount rate. "Household wealth" = SCF net worth + private defined-benefit pension wealth. This is the person-weighted within-age-group sorting; other sortings in the paper shift levels but not trends (all 16 rows are in the underlying CSV). Sources — every series, with exact citation

How this chart was produced.
