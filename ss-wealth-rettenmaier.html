Does counting Social Security change the wealth-inequality story?

Top-10% / top-5% share of US wealth by SCF wave, 1989–2019 — conventional net worth vs. a measure that includes accrued Social Security — Rettenmaier & Jansen (2022)

Standard wealth statistics count only marketable net worth and exclude the value of future Social Security benefits — on the logic that workers have no legal claim to them. But accrued benefits are large (~$41 trillion in 2019) and, because they matter proportionally far more to lower- and middle-wealth families, leaving them out makes wealth look more concentrated and rising faster.

Rettenmaier & Jansen recompute top wealth shares treating accrued Social Security as wealth, for every Survey of Consumer Finances wave. The top 10% hold a roughly stable ~17.8% of Social Security benefits but a much larger share of marketable wealth. The result: on conventional net worth the top-10% share rose 63.1% → 70.8% (1989–2019), but counting Social Security it rose only 54.0% → 57.3% — cutting the measured increase by more than half (+7.7pp → +3.3pp). For the top 5% the increase falls from +8.6pp to +4.0pp.

"Net worth" here already includes accrued defined-benefit pensions; Social Security wealth = present value of accrued benefits discounted at the 20-year forward TIPS rate. Families sorted by net worth in both series. The paper's Table 3 also breaks these series out by age group (25–54, 55–64, 65–74, 75+) — all in the underlying CSV.
